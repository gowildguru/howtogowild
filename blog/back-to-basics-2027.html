Back To “Basics”: How Airlines and Their Evolving Loyalty Programs Are Competing for Your Dollars in 2027

Airline Comparisons · Review draft · Prepared October 15, 2026

Southwest Airlines announced sweeping changes to its Rapid Rewards loyalty program today (October 15, 2026). And less than a month ago, Frontier Airlines announced costly changes to its loyalty program—requiring members to purchase bundles at an added cost to receive certain seating benefits they previously received through status. The question remains: which airline comes out on top when it comes to finding value for your air travel needs?

I can’t say that I’m surprised by the changes Southwest announced. Southwest lost its value edge last year amid pressure from activist investor Elliott. Benefits loyal customers expected and “luved” were taken away. Two free checked bags are no longer universal, with exemptions now tied to things like status, fare type and credit card membership—and open seating was replaced with assigned seating. With this push to be like the “Big Guys” came the push toward a more premium experience. We’ve already seen the introduction of Extra Legroom seats, free Wi-Fi for Rapid Rewards members where available, in-seat power on equipped aircraft, elevated snacks, interline agreements with carriers around the world, and new fare types. Southwest has also announced upcoming enhancements to its brand and customer experience with new status tiers, Southwest “Icon” Lounges, and a premium credit card. While Southwest left its identity as a low-cost carrier behind in the chase for profits, it didn’t erase its entire customer experience. Southwest still rewards customers with perks like a free carry-on, no cancellation fees, an inflight snack and nonalcoholic beverage on eligible flights, and applicable Rapid Rewards perks on all fare types.

Frontier’s loyalty squeeze

Conversely, Frontier was faced with an identity crisis: remain the country’s ultra-low-cost leader or give in to the greed of other airlines’ practices and demand more money from its already loyal customer base. Well, we all know how that ended… From my own observations, beyond the announced loyalty program changes, I’ve seen sneaky, unannounced changes that are squeezing loyal customers even more.

  • Row 3 on the flights I checked is now sold as UpFront Plus, no longer a premium seat bookable with current elite perks, removing six premium seats from those maps.
  • Discount Den is worthless in my sample. Across over a dozen flights on multiple dates and multiple routes, Discount Den saves a whopping one dollar.
  • The cash prices for Economy bundles on GoWild! fares were consistently higher in my searches than the bundle prices for other fare types.

This is on top of announced changes. So not only will you be forced to buy a bundled fare type to get certain seating perks, but in the GoWild! searches I compared, you would be charged more for that bundle than someone on another fare type. It feels like the airline knows you’ll pay it.

It almost feels as if some executive at Frontier said, “Corner them into paying us more, and since they’re loyal, let’s charge them more!” (as they sneered and rubbed their greedy hands together).

Does Frontier still present great value at times? Yes! But in such a competitive landscape, at a certain point you have to ask when the abysmal travel experience they offer will outweigh the cost savings. With new “premium-looking” branding, an app that still gives me errors and glitches, and a new loyalty program, Frontier is coming in hot with a “Premium Experience”—but, in my experience, fails to deliver when you actually show up at the airport or need customer support.

Comparison notes and sources

Cancellation: Southwest Basic is nonrefundable. Cancel at least ten minutes before the original scheduled departure to preserve any eligible credit; Basic credits normally expire six months from original booking, potentially earlier when older funds are used. No fee does not mean cash back. Frontier’s published $149 Basic cancellation fee applies to bookings made on or after September 25, 2026, subject to exceptions. If that fee exceeds the ticket’s value, no credit remains; don’t treat it as a separate $149 bill on top of a $79 fare. Qualifying 24-hour cancellations and airline-caused disruptions can have different refund rules. See Southwest Basic terms, credit expiration and Frontier cancellation terms. Frontier’s general FAQ still lists eligible elite waivers; its announced January rules remove the blanket elite waiver, so check the rules attached to your booking.

Status still counts on Frontier Basic. Under the announced rules, Gold includes a carry-on for the member; Platinum includes carry-ons and one checked bag per traveler on the booking; Diamond includes carry-ons and two checked bags per traveler. Silver does not include a carry-on. Seat upgrades require a bundle, with availability-dependent UpFront Plus/First Class windows of 4, 8, 18 and 24 hours for Silver, Gold, Platinum and Diamond, respectively. That is a narrower criticism than saying Basic loses every elite perk. Frontier Miles terms.

Southwest tiers are not interchangeable. A-List becomes Silver, A-List Preferred becomes Gold, and Platinum is new on January 1. The seating distinctions in the table matter: Silver’s Extra Legroom access starts within 48 hours of departure, rather than being an automatic check-in upgrade. Every seat benefit remains subject to availability. Southwest’s 2027 tier benefits.

Points are earned on eligible spend. The table compares the base-member earning rates without a credit card. Multiplying the entire displayed fare by 2 or 1 would overstate earnings if it includes taxes or other ineligible charges. Without the fare breakdowns, I’m not assigning exact point totals. See Southwest’s earning rules and Frontier’s eligible-purchase rules.

Onboard amenities: Southwest refreshments are subject to flight eligibility and service conditions; free Wi-Fi requires Rapid Rewards membership and availability. Frontier currently sells refreshments and lists no Wi-Fi or electrical outlets. I’ve left unverified menu prices out of the comparison. See Southwest’s menu, Wi-Fi terms and Frontier’s inflight information.

At the sample prices above, Frontier starts $59 cheaper. Add the $69 carry-on price I found, though, and that $79 fare becomes $148—$10 more than Southwest’s $138. That’s illustrative math using the sample prices, not a matched-flight quote. If your status already covers the bag, or you only need a personal item, your math will look different. Frontier’s Bag Price Checker.

So, which airline comes out on top? For me, it comes down to what I’m actually getting for my money. Frontier can still win on price. But if I have to buy a bundle just to use seating perks I already earned, while Southwest includes more of what I need, how much am I really saving? Loyalty should count for something—even when I’m getting back to “Basics.”
