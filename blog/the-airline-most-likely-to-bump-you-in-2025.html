The Airline Most Likely to Bump You in 2025

Airline News · Published May 18, 2026 · Q4 2025 data

You’ve probably seen the viral videos: gate agents offering thousands of dollars in travel credits, free upgrades, or gift cards while pleading for volunteers to take a later flight because the plane has been oversold. One report circulating on social media described an Athens-bound American Airlines flight offering passengers a business-class upgrade and as much as $4,500 to give up their seats. But what happens when nobody volunteers?

Overselling flights is a standard industry practice in the United States. Airlines use historical data and predictive modeling to estimate how many passengers will no-show, cancel, or misconnect, then sell more tickets than there are physical seats. The goal is to have flights depart as full as possible. But those calculations can go wrong—and Frontier Airlines stands out in the latest quarterly figures.

In the fourth quarter of 2025, Frontier was the only major airline network in DOT’s marketing-carrier table with more involuntary than voluntary denied boardings. Of its 2,273 total denied boardings, 755 were voluntary and 1,518 were involuntary. That produced an involuntary bumping rate of 1.82 per 10,000 passengers.

The American Airlines network—including branded regional partners—ranked next at 0.64 per 10,000. Frontier’s rate was nearly three times as high. This comparison covers October–December 2025, not the whole year. DOT Q4 2025 oversales data.

Federal rules under 14 CFR Part 250 require airlines to seek volunteers before denying boarding involuntarily. Voluntary offers are negotiated; they do not have the same mandatory payment formula as involuntary bumping. That helps explain why airlines sometimes make such eye-catching offers. DOT’s bumping guide.

Delta’s network reported zero involuntary denied boardings in Q4 2025. Southwest reported 30, for a rate of 0.01 per 10,000 passengers. Those figures contrast sharply with Frontier’s. They do not, by themselves, tell us how much each airline offered volunteers. DOT Q4 2025 oversales data.

Southwest provides another useful comparison. Its customer guidance describes offering LUV vouchers to passengers willing to change their plans when an oversale is anticipated. That gives the airline a way to seek volunteers before the situation reaches an involuntary denial. Southwest’s oversold-flight guidance.

So why is Frontier so much more likely to involuntarily deny boarding? One possible explanation is the economics of low fares. Large voluntary offers are expensive, while mandatory involuntary compensation is tied to the fare and the length of the arrival delay. That creates a potential difference in financial incentives—but the DOT figures alone do not establish Frontier’s motive.

For eligible domestic passengers facing longer delays, the required payment can be 400% of the one-way fare, subject to a $2,150 cap. Shorter qualifying delays use 200%, capped at $1,075. Eligibility exceptions apply, and arriving within one hour generally means no payment is required. For international flights departing the U.S., the longer-delay threshold differs. 14 CFR 250.5.

Here’s a simplified example: if the fare used for compensation—including mandatory taxes and fees—were $19, then 400% would be $76. A $4,500 voluntary offer is roughly equivalent to 59 such payments. That is a hypothetical comparison, not a calculation of Frontier’s actual costs. It excludes rebooking and other expenses, and an advertised $19 fare may not be the fare used for compensation.

Zero-fare tickets have a special rule: compensation is calculated using the lowest cash, check, or credit-card fare in the same class of service on that flight. A zero-dollar fare does not automatically mean zero compensation. 14 CFR 250.5.

We’re left with a few challenging questions. Should airlines face stricter limits on involuntary denied boardings? Should there be mandatory minimum compensation high enough to encourage volunteers before passengers are denied boarding against their will? Should the U.S. move toward a compensation system that is less dependent on the fare paid?

Source note: Q4 figures were checked against DOT’s report revised May 28, 2026, after this article’s original publication date.
